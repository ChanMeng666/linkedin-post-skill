# The writing system

How Chan's posts should read, on any platform. Read this every run, before drafting.

It was built by studying about twenty recent posts each from two LinkedIn writers Chan likes
reading (October 2026):

- a marketer who writes from lived moments. The source of the **Story** register.
- a founder and community organiser who writes as someone running things and reporting what he
  found. The source of the **Operator** register.

What is recorded here is craft: how they open, pace, prove and close. Their sentences, anecdotes
and the people in their posts are theirs. Never reuse any of it. Every example line below was
written for this file, in Chan's subject area, to show a shape.

The goal of every post: take one of Chan's ideas and make it **credible** (a specialist nods),
**plain** (a smart person outside the field follows it in one read) and **worth finishing** (each
paragraph earns the next).

## The rule that comes before style

**Only real material.** Both writers work because the details are true: the exact time, the real
number, the thing someone actually said. A post built on an invented anecdote, a made-up quote or
a rounded-up number is worse than no post, and Chan is the one who has to stand behind it.

- If the draft needs a moment, a number or a quote Chan hasn't given, ask her for it. One question,
  as specific as you can make it: "What did the student actually say?" beats "any more detail?".
- If she doesn't have it, write the post without it. Do not fill the gap.
- Research can supply facts about the outside world (what a release contains, who spoke). It
  cannot supply Chan's experience.

## What both writers do

These hold in both registers and on both platforms.

1. **Open with a sentence, not a headline.** No title line, no leading emoji, no colon-and-subtitle.
   The first line is something Chan would say out loud to a colleague, and it already contains the
   tension or the claim.
2. **Let specifics do the persuading.** An exact figure, a date, a place, a product name. "A while
   ago" becomes "in March". "Much faster" becomes "four days, down from three weeks". If there is
   no specific available, the sentence is probably not needed.
3. **Write short paragraphs.** One to three sentences. A paragraph of one short line marks a turn
   in the post, so use it for the turn and not for decoration.
4. **Use plain words.** Contractions, everyday verbs, "pretty" and "a bit" where Chan would say
   them. Precise nouns stay (see `brand.md`), but each one gets explained in passing the first
   time: "an MCP server, the adapter that lets a model call your tools".
5. **Admit something.** A doubt, a wrong first guess, the part that went badly. Both writers do
   this in nearly every post and it is most of why they are believed.
6. **Say one thing.** One idea per post. A second good idea is the next post.
7. **Earn the point, then state it once.** The lesson arrives after the story or the evidence, in
   one or two sentences. It is not announced at the top and it is not repeated in three wordings.
8. **Credit people for what they did.** "Thanks to Sam for rewriting the eval harness the night
   before" and never a bare list of names.
9. **Treat emoji as punctuation.** None to four per post, at the end of a sentence, carrying a
   feeling the words already set up. A single marker type may bullet a list of people or times.
   Emoji never number points, never open the post, never stand in for a word.
10. **Go easy on hashtags.** None, or three to five plain ones on the last line (LinkedIn). See
    `hashtag-bank.md`.
11. **Land the ending.** Close on a line that finishes the thought, or on a specific, low-pressure
    invitation. Never ask for engagement in general.
12. **Use commas, colons and full stops.** Neither writer leans on dashes. No em dashes.
13. **NZ English spelling.** Organise, realise, colour, programme.

## Register A: Story

Use when the idea came out of something that happened to Chan: a conversation, a failure, an
evening at an event, a small moment that changed her mind.

**Shape**

1. **Opening line with a turn inside it.** What happened, plus a hint that it didn't go the
   expected way.
2. **The scene.** A time anchor ("last March", "the night before the demo"), then the concrete
   details in order. Two to four short paragraphs. Include one line of real speech if there is one.
3. **The turn.** One short paragraph, often one line, where the expected outcome flips.
4. **What Chan noticed.** Her honest read, including what she got wrong or nearly did.
5. **Widening out.** The same point, moved from her case to the reader's. This is where it becomes
   an insight and not a diary entry. One or two short paragraphs.
6. **The callback.** A last line that returns to a concrete object or phrase from the scene.

**Techniques**

- **Time anchor first.** Starting a paragraph with when it happened makes the reader settle in.
- **The honest aside.** A short admission that slightly undercuts the narrator: "Part of me was
  hoping the build would fail so I could go home."
- **Quoted speech.** One real line, in quotation marks, does more than a paragraph of summary.
- **The run of three.** Three parallel sentences, each on its own line, to widen the point:
  "You can ship fast and still be careful. / You can use the tool and still know the craft. /
  You can be new here and still be right." Once per post at most.
- **The borrowed frame.** Describing one world in the vocabulary of another for a light laugh: a
  weekend chore written up as a sprint retro. Works once, briefly.
- **Brackets for the aside.** A parenthetical is where the humour lives.
- **The object callback.** Whatever small physical thing appeared in the scene (the sticky note,
  the 4pm coffee, the error message) comes back in the final line.

**What not to carry over.** Stacked praise words ("incredible, amazing, wonderful"), "honestly"
more than once, a closing paragraph that restates the moral, and thank-you lists longer than five
names. These are the weak spots of the form, not its strengths.

## Register B: Operator

Use when the idea is a claim Chan can back with something she built, measured, ran or read
closely: a result, a way of working, a view on where a technology is going.

**Shape**

1. **Opening line.** A short thesis, a number with its comparison, or the question the post
   answers.
2. **What Chan actually did or saw.** Dated and counted. This is the evidence, so it comes early.
3. **Why it matters.** The mechanism, in plain words. Longer posts may split here under bare
   label lines ("How we got here", "What that looks like in practice", "The lesson"): plain text,
   a line of their own, no emoji, no colon. Short posts need no labels.
4. **One worked example.** A single concrete instance of the claim in action.
5. **The lesson, as a contrast pair.** Two short sentences where the second turns the first.
6. **A specific invitation or a line that stays.** Who should get in touch and about what, or a
   closing sentence the reader will repeat to someone.

**Techniques**

- **The short thesis.** Five to eight words that could be disagreed with: "Every agent needs a
  budget before it needs a prompt."
- **Then and now.** A number is only interesting next to the number it replaced: "41 pull requests
  merged this fortnight, up from 12."
- **Name your things.** A name for the system, the habit or the pattern gives the reader a handle
  and gives the post something to be remembered by.
- **The staccato run.** Three or four one-line paragraphs with the same grammar, building to a
  point: "It doesn't need an account. / It doesn't need permission. / It doesn't need to be
  good." Once per post.
- **The contrast pair.** "The model is rented. The context is yours." State the thing everyone
  watches, then the thing that matters.
- **Define in passing.** One clause, the first time a term appears, for the reader who is new.
- **Own the flaws in public.** Say what broke, what ran late, what feedback stung, and what
  changes next time. A numbered result followed by an honest shortfall reads as a report; a
  numbered result alone reads as an advert.
- **Quote the evidence.** A line of real feedback or a real log entry, verbatim, beats a summary.
- **Functional markers only.** One marker type for a list of speakers or sessions, a calendar
  emoji on a date line. That is the full emoji budget for the body.

**What not to carry over.** Posts that are only logistics (date, time, link) with no idea in
them. An announcement still needs a reason a stranger would care.

## Choosing a register

| The raw material is… | Register |
|----------------------|----------|
| something that happened to her, and what it changed | Story |
| a result, a build, a measurement, a method | Operator |
| a reaction to an article or release | Operator, short |
| an event she attended | Story for the spine, Operator for the facts (numbers, who spoke, what was said) |
| an idea with no moment and no evidence behind it yet | Ask Chan for one or the other before drafting |

A post can borrow across the line: an Operator post often opens with two sentences of scene, and
a Story post is stronger with one hard number in it. Pick one as the spine and let the other
season it.

## Openers

Shapes for the first line. The examples are placeholders for a pattern; write a new one from
Chan's actual material every time.

| Shape | Example of the shape |
|-------|----------------------|
| Outcome with a turn | "I failed the interview, and it was the most useful hour of my month." |
| Time anchor plus trouble | "Three weeks ago our agent deleted its own test suite." |
| Short thesis | "Every team is going to need an eval before it needs an agent." |
| The reader's question | "What happens to code review when nobody typed the code?" |
| Number with its comparison | "Last sprint we merged 41 pull requests. The one before, 12." |
| Plain confession | "I've been explaining MCP wrong for a year." |
| Something overheard | "A student asked me how to hide that her portfolio was built with AI." |

## Closers

| Shape | Example of the shape |
|-------|----------------------|
| Object callback (Story) | "The sticky note is still on my monitor." |
| Contrast pair (Operator) | "The typing got cheap. The deciding didn't." |
| Specific invitation | "If you're wiring evals into a small team right now, I'd like to compare notes." |
| The question left open | "Somebody is going to ask the model that. It may as well be you." |
| Plain next step | "We run it again in December. I'll post the numbers." |

A question to the reader is fine when it is one Chan wants answered and a reader could answer in
a sentence from their own experience. "What's your take?" is neither.

## Never

These mark a post as templated or machine-written. Several were the old house style of this
skill; they are retired.

- A headline as the first line, with or without emoji.
- "Here's the framework", "Here's what I learned", "Let me break it down", "Let that sink in".
- Emoji-numbered points, `✅/❌` blocks, stacks of `→` lines, 👇 pointing at anything.
- Capitals for emphasis, and bold for emphasis. Bold on LinkedIn is for signposts only (label
  lines and lead-ins; rules in `platforms.md` → "Bold on LinkedIn"). A word that needs bold to
  land is a sentence that needs rewriting.
- "Stop doing X. Start doing Y." as a punch line.
- "What's your take? Drop your thoughts below."
- A wall of eight or more hashtags.
- Em dashes. "It's not just X, it's Y" more than once in a post.
- Vocabulary nobody says aloud: game-changer, unlock, supercharge, delve, landscape, journey (for
  anything that isn't travel), "in today's fast-paced world", plus the avoid list in `brand.md`.
- Praise words in stacks, and "excited/thrilled/humbled to announce".
- A summary paragraph that repeats what the post already said.
- Every paragraph the same length. Both writers vary: a three-sentence paragraph, then a line.

## The plain-language pass

After the draft, before Chan sees it. This is what makes a technical idea readable without
making it wrong.

1. **Read it aloud.** Any sentence Chan wouldn't say to a person across a table gets rewritten.
2. **One new term per paragraph**, explained in six words or fewer the first time.
3. **Swap abstractions for actions.** "Improved observability" becomes "we could finally see which
   step failed".
4. **Check each claim has its proof beside it.** A number, a link, a named example. If not, soften
   the claim or cut it.
5. **Cut the warm-up.** If the post still works with the first paragraph deleted, delete it.
6. **Cut the repeat.** If the last paragraph says what an earlier one said, keep the better one.
7. **Test the reader outside the field.** Would a marketer, a founder or a nurse who follows Chan
   get the point? They don't need every detail. They need to know what happened and why it matters.

Then run `node scripts/count.mjs output/<date>-<slug>/post.md`. It checks length for each platform
and flags most of the "Never" list mechanically.
